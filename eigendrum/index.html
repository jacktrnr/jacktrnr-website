Can you hear the shape of a drum? Partly. Add up e−λt over every mode to get the heat trace Z(t). For short times, 4πt·Z(t) ≈ area − (√π/2)·perimeter·√t + 4π·c·t. So the curve starts at the area, its slope gives the perimeter, and its bend gives c, which counts holes and corners. Here those are read from the eigenvalues alone and checked against the real shape. Modes beyond the ones computed are filled in from the fit. (Some different shapes do share a spectrum, so the answer is “partly”.)

nodal domains of each modeCourant: at most kPleijel: eventually at most 0.69 krandom waves: about 0.062 k

The nodal lines cut each mode into regions that move together, alternating red and blue. Courant proved mode k has at most k of them. Pleijel showed that only finitely many modes reach k exactly (they are called Courant-sharp), and that in the long run the count stays below 69% of k. Chaotic shapes behave like random waves, about 6% of k.

Each dot is one bounce of a classical ball: where on the wall, and at what angle. The color is the selected mode, written in those same coordinates. Each mode is a pattern the drum can vibrate in all by itself, at one pitch. Red and blue parts move in opposite directions; along the nodal lines the drum stays still. Every hit is a mix of these patterns, which is what the Drum tab plays.

Keyboard: ← → step through the modes, ↓ jumps ahead 10 and ↑ jumps back 10. Each step plays the mode's note. You can also click a mode in the strip below, or use ◀ ▶. Higher modes have more nodal lines and higher pitch, roughly in proportion to √λ.

Regular billiards trace smooth curves here (try the ellipse). Chaotic ones scatter dots over the whole plane (try the stadium). Mixed ones show islands in a chaotic sea (try the mushroom). It is the classical side of the spacing statistics on the Modes tab.

Chaos meter: a ghost ball (hollow ring) starts one billionth away from ball 1. How fast they drift apart, against bounces, on a log scale

In a chaotic billiard the gap grows by the same factor every bounce, a straight line on this log plot. Its slope is the Lyapunov exponent. In a regular one the gap only grows in proportion to the number of bounces, which bends over flat. When the gap gets large the ghost restarts next to the ball, so you get a sawtooth.

A quantum billiard: a particle trapped in the shape, iψt = −Δψ with ψ = 0 on the edge. Click the drum to drop a wave packet; drag before letting go to give it momentum. When the computed modes hold the whole packet, ψ evolves exactly in them. Otherwise the full grid or mesh is stepped in time, and it switches over once enough modes have arrived.

Wave packet

Survival probability: how much of the starting state is still there, |⟨ψ(0)|ψ(t)⟩|²

The packet leaves its starting spot, so this drops fast. Echoes come back when parts of the wave return along short classical orbits. Over a long time it settles to Σ|ck|⁴ (dashed), where ck is how much of mode k the start contains. Starting from a single mode, it stays at 1.

What my undergrad thesis did

In chapter 6 of my undergrad thesis I put the shape inside a box and built its modes out of the box’s sine waves. Everything outside the shape gets a large penalty V₀. The integrals over each pixel only have to be computed once, so changing the shape is cheap.

H_nm = λ_n δ_nm + V₀ · (sum of pixel integrals outside the shape) eig(H) → the drum’s modes, as V₀ → ∞

The downside is a large table, a dense matrix, and an error that only shrinks like 1/√V₀.

Pixel shapes, done faster

  • Let V₀ go all the way to infinity. The pixels outside the shape then drop out of the problem.
  • Use the pixels themselves as the building blocks. What remains is a sparse matrix with five entries per row.
  • Only ask for the lowest modes, and start from the last answer while you draw. A brush stroke usually takes a few milliseconds.

Smooth shapes

  • Round shapes use Bessel functions: the disk, quarter disk, semicircle and annulus.
  • Three triangles (equilateral, right isosceles and 30-60-90) have exact answers built from sums of plane waves, a trick that goes back to Lamé.
  • The other shapes from my undergrad thesis have no formula. They are solved with finite elements on a mesh that follows the real edge.

Either way the edge is drawn as a smooth curve, not as pixel steps.

Sound and heat

  • A hit is a small bump of width σ. Mode k is excited in proportion to e−λkσ²/2, so a wider hammer reaches fewer high modes and sounds darker. This is the dashed curve on the chart. Modes past λ = 18.4/σ² are below −80 dB and are left out.
  • Exact shapes add up every mode the hit reaches, often more than a thousand. Pixel and mesh shapes use the modes computed so far. They are found in the background, lowest first, and each hit uses all the ones that are ready.
  • Across the room is what a microphone a few meters away hears. Sound comes from the air the drum pushes, so modes whose up and down halves cancel are almost silent. You hear fewer notes and a sharper attack.
  • Contact mic is a sensor stuck to the drum where you hit it. It picks up every mode the hit excites, weighted toward the low ones, so it sounds darker and fuller.
  • For heat, the edges stay at 0° and each source stays at its own temperature. “Steady state” jumps straight to where the temperature settles.
  • The Schrödinger tab solves iψt = −Δψ for a particle trapped in the shape. A wave packet moves, spreads, bounces off the edges and interferes with itself. A single mode just turns its phase, so its |ψ|² never changes.
