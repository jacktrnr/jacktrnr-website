The thesis method: partitioned expansion

Embed the shape in a box S with a known sine basis φn, and put a wall V₀ on every pixel outside Ω′. Chapter 6 precomputes the pixel integrals once, so changing the shape only re-weights them:

h_nm,jk = ∫_{P_jk} φ_n φ_m dr (M × M × J × K array) H_nm = λ_n δ_nm + V₀ Σ_{P_jk ⊄ Ω′} h_nm,jk eig(H) → λ_k(Ω′), V₀ → ∞, M → ∞

Cost: an M²·J·K table, a dense M×M eigensolve, an error of order V₀−1/2, and round-off when V₀ is large. The paper settles on V₀ ≈ 10⁵–10⁶ as a trade-off.

What this app does instead

  1. Factor the table. φn(x,y) = sn₁(x)sn₂(y), so hnm,jk = an₁m₁,j·bn₂m₂,k. Two small 1D tables replace the 4D array. The "vs thesis" tab assembles H this way in O(JKN² + KN⁴).
  2. Take V₀ → ∞ exactly. The eigenvalues rise monotonically in V₀ to the Dirichlet values on Ω′. In that limit the wall simply removes the outside pixels from the problem, so the V₀ error and the round-off trade-off go away.
  3. Use the pixels as the basis. With M = J·K sine modes, sampling φn on the pixels is a discrete sine transform, and it diagonalizes the box's 5-point Laplacian. Deleting the outside pixels leaves a sparse 5-point Laplacian on Ω′. The boundary sits on the pixel edges (ghost value −u), so Ω′ is exactly the union of pixels from eq. (6.1). Storage is one entry per pixel, and a matrix-vector product costs about 5 flops per pixel.
  4. Solve only for the low end. A Chebyshev-filtered block subspace iteration finds the lowest K modes. It starts from a half-resolution solve and warm-restarts from the previous modes while you draw, so a stroke typically costs a few iterations.

As Ω′ → Ω in the Hausdorff sense, λk(Ω′) → λk(Ω) (Chenais; thesis §6.1). Wave, heat and drum all use the same operator: utt = Δu (leapfrog), ut = Δu (explicit Euler), and the modal sum Σ φk(x₀) φk(x) e−γkt cos(2πfkt).
